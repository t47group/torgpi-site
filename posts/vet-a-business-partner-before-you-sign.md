Most business disasters don't start with bad luck. They start with trust extended too quickly — to a partner, an investor, a buyer or a vendor whose past nobody checked.

Due diligence isn't about assuming the worst. It's about confirming the story before you bet on it.

## The checklist

### 1. Identity

Confirm the person is who they say they are. Full legal name, prior names and past addresses are the foundation for everything else.

### 2. Business history

- What companies have they owned, managed or served as an officer of?
- Are those companies active, dissolved or in bad standing?
- Do their claimed successes show up in public filings, press or customer reviews?

### 3. Litigation

Search civil court records in every county and state where they've lived or done business. Look for:

- Lawsuits by partners, investors, customers or employees
- Fraud or breach-of-contract claims
- Patterns — one lawsuit is normal; ten similar ones is a story

### 4. Liens, judgments and bankruptcies

Unpaid judgments and tax liens tell you how someone handles obligations when things go wrong.

### 5. Licenses and regulatory actions

If they claim a professional license — contractor, real estate, securities, insurance — verify it with the licensing body and check for discipline.

### 6. Reputation and open sources

News coverage, online reviews, social media and industry chatter round out the picture.

### 7. References you find yourself

Anyone can supply three glowing references. Talk to a former partner or customer you located independently.

## Red flags that should slow you down

- Pressure to commit quickly, before you can check anything
- Reluctance to provide full legal names or company details
- A track record that can't be verified anywhere
- Previous ventures that ended in lawsuits with partners
