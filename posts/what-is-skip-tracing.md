The term comes from the old phrase "skipping town." Today, skip tracing simply means locating someone whose whereabouts are unknown — a debtor, a witness, an heir, an old friend, a family member.

## Who uses skip tracing

- **Creditors and landlords** trying to collect a debt or judgment
- **Attorneys** who need to find a witness or serve a party
- **Estates and trusts** searching for heirs or beneficiaries
- **Businesses** recovering property or reconnecting with former contacts
- **Families** trying to find a relative they've lost touch with

## How it works

Good skip tracing is a layered process, not a single database search.

1. **Confirm identity.** Common names produce dozens of matches. Date of birth, past addresses and known associates narrow it to the right person.
2. **Research records.** Licensed investigators have access to investigative data sources not available to the public, alongside court records, property records, business filings and licensing data.
3. **Search open sources.** Social media, online reviews, business listings and news can reveal a current city, employer or relationship.
4. **Verify in the field.** Data goes stale. A drive-by or discreet in-person check confirms that a person actually lives or works where the records say.
5. **Report.** You receive a verified address, phone number or place of employment — and how confident we are in it.

## Why instant "people search" sites fall short

Online people-search sites are inexpensive, but they often merge different people, list addresses that are years old and miss anyone who has deliberately gone quiet. That's fine for curiosity. It's not fine when you're about to file a lawsuit, serve papers or knock on a door.

## What skip tracing won't do

Licensed investigators don't obtain phone, bank or utility records through deception — that kind of pretexting is illegal. And we may decline a locate if we have reason to believe the person is avoiding contact for their own safety.

## How long does it take?

Many locates are resolved within a few days. Someone who has intentionally disappeared may take longer and require field work.
